GHK-Cu: a research guide to the copper peptide and its documentation

A naturally occurring tripeptide that binds copper, widely used in cosmetic ingredient research and studied in cell and tissue models.

Research-use-only guide. It describes the compound and how to assess supplier documentation, and offers no usage instructions or health claims. Supplier links are affiliate links; see the note further down.

A blue peptide with a long history

In the early 1970s, a biochemist named Loren Pickart noticed something curious while studying liver tissue. Cells from older donors responded poorly to a growth signal that cells from younger donors handled well, and something in young blood plasma seemed to correct that. He isolated the active factor and found it was a tiny peptide, just three amino acids long: glycine, histidine and lysine. It was clutching a copper ion. That compound, GHK-Cu, has been studied ever since, mostly in cell cultures and skin models, and it now sits in a unique spot: it is a research peptide, and also an ingredient in many cosmetic products.

That dual identity is the first thing to understand about it. When people say GHK-Cu is well researched, they are right in a narrow sense. There is a large literature on its behavior in vitro: effects on fibroblast activity, collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis in culture, and gene expression changes catalogued in transcriptomic databases. But the leap from cell culture to claims about people is where marketing tends to run ahead of evidence, and this site takes no position on any therapeutic or cosmetic effect.

Two components, not one

Most compounds in this directory are a single, well-defined chain. GHK-Cu is a complex: a tripeptide plus a metal ion held in a particular geometry. The peptide alone, written GHK, has a mass around 340 daltons. Bound to copper(II), the complex weighs roughly 400. The two behave differently, they are used differently, and they are priced differently, but the wording on vendor pages is often loose.

The histidine side chain does most of the work of grabbing the copper, with help from the free amino terminus and the peptide backbone. The binding is strong, which is why the complex forms spontaneously in the presence of copper and why it is found naturally circulating in blood plasma and saliva. It is also why the powder is such a vivid blue. That colour is not a coincidence or an additive. It is the visible signature of copper(II) in this coordination environment, and it is the cheapest quality check you will ever get.

The colour test, and what it can and cannot tell you

A genuine GHK-Cu cake should be a clear, medium to deep blue. White, grey or pale blue-green material raises questions. Possible explanations include free GHK sold under the wrong name, insufficient copper, or contamination. A very dark or greenish product may suggest excess copper salts. None of this replaces analysis, but the colour is evidence you can see the moment the vial arrives. If a listing photograph shows a white powder, it is not showing GHK-Cu.

The test has limits. A blue powder tells you copper is present, not that the peptide is the right one or that it is pure. A lab can make blue material by mixing copper salt with almost anything containing a histidine. This is why a proper certificate of analysis should add two further layers of evidence on top of the colour.

What a convincing GHK-Cu COA contains

Peptide identityMass spectrometry showing the expected mass. For the complex, look for the copper isotope pattern; for free GHK, a peak near 340.
Purity by HPLCA chromatogram of the peptide. Because the complex can partly dissociate during analysis, a thoughtful lab will say how it handled this.
Copper contentAn assay such as ICP or atomic absorption. This is the distinguishing test, and it is missing from many reports. Without it, you know about the peptide but not the metal.
Batch detailsLot number matching your vial, a test date, and a named laboratory.
Counter-ionsMention of acetate or other anions if the lab measured them, since they affect how much of the powder is active material.

Many sellers publish an HPLC chart and a mass but nothing about copper. That is a gap, not a disqualification. It is reasonable to email and ask whether a copper assay exists for the lot. Suppliers that run the test usually say so proudly, and those that do not may simply not have thought about it.

If a vial arrives the wrong colour

Photograph it immediately, in daylight, next to the label and the lot number, and keep the packaging. Then contact the supplier with the images and ask for the batch COA and their policy. Reputable suppliers treat this as a normal quality query: they ask for the lot number, compare it against retained samples, and either explain the discrepancy or replace the product. A supplier that responds with silence, with a generic reassurance, or with a refusal to engage is telling you how it will behave when something more serious goes wrong. This is the kind of response pattern we weigh when reviewing suppliers, and it is the reason we encourage buyers to read refund and complaint policies before ordering, not after. Dark, greenish or white-and-patchy material is worth a query; a slight batch-to-batch shade variation is not unusual and need not alarm you on its own.

Cosmetic grade versus research grade

Because GHK-Cu is a common cosmetic ingredient, search results mix products with very different purposes. Cosmetic raw material is manufactured and documented to cosmetic-ingredient standards, with its own regulatory expectations. Research-grade vials sold by the suppliers on this site are labelled for laboratory use only and sit outside those expectations. The two should not be assumed to be interchangeable, and a COA written for one purpose does not necessarily answer the questions relevant to the other. This directory covers the research-supply side exclusively and offers no guidance on topical or other use.

Stability notes in documentation terms

Copper-peptide complexes are somewhat more sensitive than plain peptides: copper can catalyse oxidation, and the complex responds to pH and to competing metal-binding agents. In practice that means the freeze-dried form is the sensible way to ship it, and suppliers should state storage conditions. It is worth checking whether the listing says anything about how long a batch has been held and under what conditions. The same questions we pose in our review methodology apply here as to any peptide.

Questions we hear

Why is it blue?

Copper(II) bound to the peptide absorbs red-orange light, which makes the complex look blue. Free GHK without copper is colourless.

Does GHK-Cu occur naturally?

The tripeptide has been reported in human plasma, saliva and urine, and is thought to be released from larger proteins. The vials sold to laboratories are synthesised.

Is it the same thing used in skincare?

The molecule is the same; concentration, formulation, grade and regulatory status are not. This page deals with laboratory supply only.
