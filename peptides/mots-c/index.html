MOTS-c: a research guide to the peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA

A 16-amino-acid peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA, studied in metabolism and aging research in cell and animal models.

Research-use-only guide. It describes what MOTS-c is and how to read supplier documents. It offers no dosing, protocols or health advice. Supplier links are affiliate links; details are below.

A message from the other genome

Nearly everything a human cell needs is encoded in the 20,000-odd genes of the nuclear genome. But each cell also carries hundreds of copies of a small, separate circle of DNA inside its mitochondria, the organelles that generate most of the cell's energy. That mitochondrial genome is tiny, about 16,500 letters, and for decades biologists believed they had catalogued everything it did: 13 proteins, 22 transfer RNAs and two ribosomal RNAs. Then, in 2015, a team at the University of Southern California led by Changhan David Lee and Pinchas Cohen reported something that did not fit. Hidden inside one of the ribosomal RNA genes, they had found a short open reading frame that cells actually translate into a peptide. They called it MOTS-c, short for mitochondrial open reading frame of the twelve S rRNA type-c.

The peptide is 16 amino acids long, and its sequence in humans is Met-Arg-Trp-Gln-Glu-Met-Gly-Tyr-Ile-Phe-Tyr-Pro-Arg-Lys-Leu-Arg. Its mass is about 2,174 daltons. The discovery mattered because it suggested mitochondria do not merely generate energy but also send signals to the rest of the cell, and possibly to other tissues, through peptides that circulate in blood.

What the published work actually shows

The original paper and the studies that followed examined MOTS-c in mice and in cell culture. In animals fed a high-fat diet, researchers reported that treatment with the peptide was associated with improved glucose handling and reduced weight gain. Later work in mice connected it to exercise, reporting that muscle and blood levels of MOTS-c rise during physical activity in both mice and people, and that older mice given the peptide performed better on endurance tests. The proposed mechanism involves activation of AMPK, an enzyme that acts as the cell's fuel gauge, and changes in folate and methionine metabolism.

It would be easy to turn that into a promotional paragraph, and many vendor pages do. It is better to be plain about the limits. The evidence is overwhelmingly preclinical. The human data consist mainly of observational measurements of the naturally occurring peptide, and one genetic association study: a variant in the mitochondrial gene that swaps lysine at position 14 for glutamine has been associated with differences in metabolic traits in some East Asian populations. A controlled human trial of administered MOTS-c, at the time of writing, is limited to early-stage work. Nothing about the compound is approved, and we do not offer advice on using it.

The methionine problem

For a buyer, the most interesting feature of MOTS-c is the sequence itself. It contains two methionines, at positions 1 and 6, and a tryptophan at position 3. Those three residues are among the most easily oxidised in the amino acid alphabet. Methionine takes up an oxygen atom to become methionine sulfoxide, adding 16 daltons to the molecule's mass. Both can occur during synthesis, during cleavage from the resin, during freeze-drying, and slowly in storage if the vial is poorly sealed or exposed to warmth.

That produces a very concrete test. A single oxidation puts a second peak in the mass spectrum at about 2,190 daltons. Two oxidations give about 2,206. On a chromatogram, oxidised forms normally run slightly earlier than the intact peptide, because the sulfoxide is more polar, producing a small peak or a shoulder on the leading edge of the main one. A well-made, well-stored batch should show a small or absent oxidation peak. A COA that quotes high purity but includes no mass spectrum cannot tell you anything about this, and an old batch of a methionine-rich peptide is exactly the case where the missing information matters.

Date matters more than usual

For stable peptides, a COA from last spring and a COA from last week tell you roughly the same thing. For an oxidation-prone peptide, they do not. A test result is a snapshot of the batch on one day, and the batch then sat in a warehouse. If a supplier displays a COA dated many months before shipment, ask whether the batch was retested. If they publish manufacturing dates, take note. Cold shipping and proper storage are decent signs of a supplier that understands the molecule it sells.

What the word "mitochondrial-derived" does and does not tell you

MOTS-c belongs to a small family of mitochondrial-derived peptides, alongside humanin and the SHLP group. The shared label describes where the genes sit, not a shared function or a shared standard of evidence. Humanin, for instance, was found a decade earlier and has been studied in the context of cell survival; MOTS-c has been studied mainly for metabolism. Researchers sometimes speak of them together because they appear to be part of one signalling system, but the individual literatures are separate, and a result reported for one should not be borrowed for another. The same applies to supplier marketing. A listing that implies MOTS-c has established human effects because it is "mitochondrial" or "natural" is leaning on the category rather than on data.

A further caution concerns measurement. Measuring natural MOTS-c in blood is difficult, and early reports of circulating levels have used assays whose specificity is still debated. When you read a paper that quotes concentrations, check which method was used and whether it has been independently validated. That is the kind of detail that rarely reaches a product page, and it is a good example of why reading primary sources, and not summaries, pays off.

How it compares to other mitochondrial compounds

MOTS-c is often mentioned together with SS-31 and with NAD+, because all three come up in discussions of mitochondrial biology. They have nothing in common chemically. SS-31 is a four-residue designer peptide that accumulates at the inner mitochondrial membrane. NAD+ is not a peptide at all but a coenzyme. MOTS-c is a natural peptide that is made inside the mitochondrion and acts as a signal. Grouping them under one banner, as some listings do, can obscure what is being sold.

Questions and answers

Is MOTS-c made in the human body?

Yes, a peptide with this sequence is produced from mitochondrial DNA. The vials sold to laboratories are chemically synthesised, not extracted.

Why does it matter that it is encoded in mitochondrial DNA?

It showed that the mitochondrial genome encodes more than the 13 proteins once thought, and suggested mitochondria communicate with the rest of the body through small peptides.

Can a COA show whether the peptide is oxidised?

Yes, if it includes a mass spectrum and a full chromatogram. A single purity percentage cannot.
