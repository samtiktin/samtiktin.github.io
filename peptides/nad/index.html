NAD+: a research guide to the coenzyme sold alongside peptides

NAD+ is a coenzyme, not a peptide. It is listed alongside peptides by most vendors, so it appears in this directory for comparison.

Research-use-only guide. It explains what NAD+ is and how to evaluate supplier documentation. It offers no dosing, administration routes or health guidance. Supplier links are affiliate links; see below.

Why a molecule that is not a peptide is on a peptide site

Look through almost any research-peptide catalog and you will find NAD+ sitting between a growth-hormone secretagogue and a copper peptide. It is not a peptide. It has no amino acids, no peptide bonds and no sequence. It is a dinucleotide, two nucleotide units joined at their phosphate groups, and it is among the oldest and most thoroughly studied molecules in biochemistry. Vendors list it with peptides because it is sold as a freeze-dried powder in a vial, to the same customers, through the same channels. This directory follows that market reality, but it is worth saying plainly that most of what we say about peptide quality control does not carry over, and some of it is actively misleading.

NAD+ stands for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. Its two halves are an adenine nucleotide and a nicotinamide nucleotide, bound through a pyrophosphate bridge. The molecular formula is C21H27N7O14P2 and the mass is about 663 daltons, which puts it in the same size range as a tetrapeptide. In the cell, it exists in two interconvertible states: the oxidised NAD+ and the reduced NADH, which carries a pair of electrons.

A century of biochemistry

The story begins in 1906, when British biochemists Arthur Harden and William Young discovered that yeast extract needed a heat-stable small molecule to ferment sugar. That cofactor turned out to be NAD+. Otto Warburg identified its role in hydrogen transfer in the 1930s, and the molecule became one of the central characters of metabolism: it shuttles electrons in glycolysis, in the citric acid cycle, and in the mitochondrial respiratory chain. For that work and its successors, several Nobel Prizes were awarded.

In recent decades NAD+ moved from textbook cofactor to a topic of intense research interest for a second reason. A set of enzymes does not use NAD+ to move electrons at all, but consumes it: the sirtuin family of deacylases, the PARP enzymes involved in DNA repair, and the surface enzyme CD38. These consumers tie cellular NAD+ supply to gene regulation, stress response and ageing biology. Studies in mice and in human tissue samples have reported that NAD+ levels fall in several tissues with age, though the magnitude is debated and varies between organs and methods. Whether raising NAD+ would be beneficial in people is the subject of ongoing clinical research, mainly using precursor molecules, and no definitive conclusions can be drawn here. We make no claims about effects.

Why quality is a different problem

For a peptide, the central questions are identity by mass and purity by chromatography. For NAD+, the concerns are rather different, and they come from the molecule's chemistry.

It is unstable. The bond linking nicotinamide to its ribose is weak, particularly in warm, acidic or alkaline conditions. Over time NAD+ breaks down into nicotinamide and ADP-ribose. The powder is also hygroscopic, meaning it pulls moisture from the air, and absorbed water accelerates the decay. A vial that has sat warm or been opened repeatedly may have lost a meaningful fraction of its content, and a purity figure from the day of manufacture will not reflect that.

The oxidation state matters. NADH, the reduced form, is a common contaminant of NAD+ preparations, and it absorbs light differently. A good certificate will report the ratio or at least the content of the oxidised form specifically.

"Purity" needs a definition. HPLC methods for nucleotides differ from peptide methods, and a COA should say what was measured and how. An assay based on an enzymatic cycling reaction can report functional content, and a UV absorbance reading at 260 nm gives a total nucleotide estimate that would include breakdown products.

It comes in several forms. NAD+ may be sold as the free acid or as a sodium salt, and the two have different molecular weights, so "500 mg" does not mean the same quantity of active molecule. Check the listing for the stated form.

IdentityMass about 663 Da for the free acid, or a UV and NMR match to a reference standard.

Shipping and storage tell you a lot

Given this fragility, packaging and storage conditions are part of the product. A supplier that ships NAD+ in sealed, desiccated, protective packaging, states storage temperature, and gives a retest date is acting as though it understands the molecule. One that ships ambient-temperature powder in a bag with no notes is gambling with yours. Because the vials are large, often hundreds of milligrams rather than five or ten, cost differences between suppliers can look dramatic. A bargain on a degraded batch is not a bargain, and a premium may reflect storage and handling. When you read our supplier reviews, look for what the supplier says about its cold chain; it is a particularly useful signal for this compound.

Comparing prices sensibly

Because NAD+ vials are sold by the hundred milligrams, it is tempting to compare cost per milligram and stop there. Three adjustments make the comparison fairer. First, convert any sodium-salt listing to a free-acid equivalent, because the counter-ion adds weight. Second, ask about water content: a hygroscopic powder at several percent moisture contains correspondingly less NAD+ per gram. Third, discount for age. A batch that is a year old and was not stored cold may carry less than its label claims, whereas a recent batch from a supplier with a retest date is closer to what the paper says. After these corrections the price gaps between suppliers often shrink, and sometimes reverse. None of this requires laboratory equipment; it only requires asking the supplier three direct questions and noticing how they answer.

Precursors are a separate world

You will see related molecules marketed alongside NAD+, mainly nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN). These are building blocks the cell can use to make NAD+, studied in human trials as oral supplements, and they have their own regulatory history. They are not the same compound, their chemistry is different, and the evidence for one does not apply to another. This page covers NAD+ only.

Short answers

Is NAD+ a peptide?

No. It is a dinucleotide coenzyme. It is listed with peptides by vendors for convenience.

Why are the vial sizes so much larger?

Researchers typically work with NAD+ in far larger quantities than with potent signalling peptides, so vials hold hundreds of milligrams.

Does a good purity figure guarantee a good vial?

Not for NAD+. It degrades with heat and moisture, so storage history and dates matter as much as the number.
