BPC-157: a research guide to the peptide and its documentation

A 15-amino-acid synthetic peptide derived from a sequence found in human gastric juice protein, studied almost entirely in rodent models.

Research-use-only material. This page describes what BPC-157 is and how to evaluate a supplier's documentation. It does not offer dosing, protocols or health advice. Some links below are affiliate links; see the note near the end.

A peptide with a small paper trail

BPC-157 is one of the most searched compounds in the research-peptide market, and also one of the least well documented in the formal literature. The name stands for "body protection compound," and the sequence, fifteen amino acids long, is described as a fragment of a larger protein found in human gastric juice. What you buy from a laboratory supplier is not extracted from anything. It is built by solid-phase chemical synthesis, cleaved from the resin, purified by preparative chromatography, and freeze-dried into a white powder.

If you read around the topic for an hour you will notice something odd. Dozens of papers exist, but a large proportion trace to the same small group of investigators in Croatia, working mostly in rats. Those papers report effects on tendon, muscle, gut lining and blood vessels in injury models. Independent replication outside that network is sparse, and the few human data points are small, uncontrolled, or both. None of this means the findings are wrong. It does mean that a careful reader should keep the evidence in proportion, and should be suspicious of anyone who describes the compound with certainty.

It also means something practical for buyers. Because there is no approved pharmaceutical version, no pharmacopoeia monograph, and no reference standard everyone agrees on, there is no external authority that will tell you whether the powder in your vial is what the label says. The supplier's own paperwork is nearly the only evidence you get. That is why this page spends most of its length on documentation rather than on biology.

The molecule at a glance

SequenceGly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val
Length15 amino acids, no unusual residues
Average massabout 1,419 Da
AppearanceWhite to off-white lyophilized cake or powder
Common salt formsAcetate (typical), arginate (sold as a more stable variant)

Fifteen residues is a comfortable size for modern synthesis. It is long enough that a poor manufacturer can leave behind deletion sequences, where one amino acid failed to couple and the chain carried on without it, but short enough that a competent one can reach very high purity. The sequence is also rich in proline, which affects how it behaves on a chromatography column. A trace with a single clean main peak and a flat baseline is realistic; a trace with a shoulder on the main peak deserves a question.

Reading a BPC-157 certificate of analysis

A certificate of analysis is a lab report for one production batch. The useful ones have four ingredients, and each tells you something different.

The HPLC trace. High-performance liquid chromatography separates the sample into components and prints a chart. You want to see the chart itself, not just a stated percentage. A number like "99.1%" is easy to type; a trace is harder to fake and lets you judge how tall the main peak is relative to everything else. Look at the baseline, any small peaks before and after the main one, and the integration notes. Deletion sequences usually elute close to the product, so a lump riding on the main peak is the thing to watch.

The mass spectrum. This answers a different question: is the main peak actually BPC-157? The expected mass is around 1,419 Da. Most reports show the multiply charged ions the instrument really detects, so do not expect to see 1,419 printed in large type; a good report will show the deconvoluted or calculated value beside the observed one. If the report shows a mass but not the sequence it was matched against, you are taking the supplier's word.

Batch identity. The report should carry a batch or lot number that matches what is printed on your vial, a test date, and the name of the testing laboratory. A COA that is undated, unnumbered, or identical across months of orders is a document, not a test.

Net peptide content. This is the item most buyers overlook. A freeze-dried peptide powder is not pure peptide; it also contains counter-ions (acetate or trifluoroacetate) and residual water. A vial labeled 10 mg might contain eight and a half milligrams of actual peptide. Some suppliers report this, many do not. When you compare per-milligram prices between suppliers, comparing an unadjusted label amount against a measured content figure is not a fair comparison.

Salt forms: acetate and arginate

You will see two forms listed. The acetate salt is the conventional product from synthesis and is what most published work used. The arginate form pairs the peptide with arginine, and sellers present it as more stable in solution. Whether that stability advantage is real in practice is not something I can verify from independent data, and I would not treat the marketing as settled fact. The relevant point for comparison shopping is simple: they are different chemical entities with different molecular weights, so match like with like before you compare anything. A COA for the acetate salt does not describe the arginate product, and vice versa.

Capsule and oral products appear in the same search results. They are a different category with different quality questions, such as excipients, and a vial COA does not transfer to them. This page concerns the lyophilized research vial only.

Storage and handling, in documentation terms

Freeze-dried peptides are generally far more stable than the same peptide in water, which is why they ship as powder. Sensible suppliers state storage conditions on the label or listing and ship in sealed vials with intact crimps. The questions worth asking are about the supply chain rather than the bench: how long did the batch sit before shipping, was it shipped cold or at ambient temperature, and how does the supplier handle a customer who reports a damaged vial. The answers show up in a supplier's policies pages, which is one reason our reviews read those closely.

Questions people ask

Is BPC-157 found naturally in the body?

The sequence is described as derived from a protein in gastric juice, but the synthetic fifteen-mer sold to laboratories is not extracted from human tissue.

Why do vials differ so much in price?

Some of the spread comes from vial size and some from purification effort, but much of it is simply margin. Without a net-content figure it is hard to say how much real peptide each vial holds, so price alone says little.

Is BPC-157 approved for any use?

No. It is not an approved drug in the United States or the European Union, and it appears on the list of substances prohibited in tested sport. This site covers research supply only and gives no guidance on use.
