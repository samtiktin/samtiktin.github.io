Eloralintide: what the trials show about Lilly's amylin drug, and what they don't

By the PeptideSuppliers.org editorial team · How we review

Eloralintide is one of the most talked-about obesity drug candidates of the past year, and one of the least understood. It is made by Eli Lilly, it works through a hormone pathway most readers have never heard of, and it is not approved anywhere. This guide explains what it is, what the published trials report, what is still unknown, and why anything sold online under its name deserves caution.

This is an educational summary of published research on an investigational drug. It is not medical advice, not a recommendation to use any product, and contains no dosing guidance. Doses mentioned below are those used in clinical trials under medical supervision.

What eloralintide is

Eloralintide (formerly LY3841136) is an investigational, once-weekly injectable selective amylin receptor agonist. Amylin is a hormone the body releases after eating, alongside insulin. It helps signal fullness and slows the emptying of the stomach. Drugs that mimic it aim to reduce appetite and food intake. Lilly describes eloralintide as designed to preferentially activate the amylin 1 receptor, and says its effect on calorie intake is likely mediated through satiety.

It is a different mechanism from the GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and the dual GIP/GLP-1 drug tirzepatide, which are the best-known obesity medicines today. That difference is the point of interest. A drug that works through amylin could be used on its own, for people who do not tolerate or respond to incretin drugs, or alongside them, to add to the effect. Lilly has pursued both paths. For background on the incretin side, see our guide to tirzepatide research and hype.

It is not the first amylin analogue. Pramlintide has been used in diabetes for years, and cagrilintide, from Novo Nordisk, is in late-stage trials combined with semaglutide. Lilly’s preclinical work compared eloralintide with cagrilintide in rats and reported lower body weight with better gastrointestinal tolerability, though that is animal data from a conference abstract and not proof of an advantage in people.

The Phase 2 monotherapy trial

The main published result is a 48-week Phase 2 trial in 263 adults with obesity, or overweight plus at least one related condition, and without type 2 diabetes. It was published in The Lancet in November 2025 and presented at ObesityWeek. Participants were randomized to placebo or to one of several weekly eloralintide dose arms. Lilly’s release reports these average weight changes using the efficacy estimand, meaning the result expected if everyone had stayed on treatment:

Placebo−0.4%
Eloralintide 1 mg−9.5%
Eloralintide 3 mg−12.4%
Eloralintide 6 mg−17.6%
Eloralintide 9 mg−20.1%
Dose escalation arms (6/9 mg and 3/6/9 mg)−19.9% and −16.4%

Those numbers are large. Two cautions apply. First, the efficacy estimand is the more favorable of the two ways of counting. Results that include people who stopped treatment are generally smaller, and Lilly’s summary does not give them. Second, Lilly notes the endpoints were not adjusted for multiplicity. Phase 2 trials are also small, so the figures can change in larger studies.

The most common side effects were gastrointestinal symptoms and fatigue, mostly mild to moderate and more frequent at higher doses. Lilly says rates were lower with slower dose escalation and similar to placebo in the 1 mg and 3 mg arms. Secondary results pointed to improvements in waist circumference, blood pressure, lipids, blood sugar and inflammation markers. Secondary outcomes from a Phase 2 study are signals, not established benefits.

The combination with tirzepatide

In September 2026, Lilly presented results at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes meeting from a 48-week Phase 2b trial of eloralintide combined with tirzepatide, which it calls EloraTZP. It enrolled 367 adults with obesity or overweight and type 2 diabetes in the United States and Argentina. Eloralintide and tirzepatide were given as separate injections. Using the efficacy estimand, average weight loss was:

Placebo−3.0%
Tirzepatide 15 mg alone−14.8%
Eloralintide alone (3, 6 and 9 mg arms)−8.2%, −12.3%, −11.1%
Highest combination (eloralintide 9 mg plus tirzepatide 15 mg)−23.3%

Lilly also reported A1C reductions of up to 2.9% for the combination, compared with 2.4% for tirzepatide alone. Two features of the data matter. The eloralintide-alone arms did less in people with diabetes (11 to 12 percent) than in the non-diabetes trial (up to 20 percent), which is a reminder that weight-loss results do not transfer cleanly between groups. And the combination was harder to tolerate: stopping treatment because of side effects ranged from 10.8% to 27.0% in the combination arms, against 2.9% for tirzepatide alone and 0% to 10.8% for eloralintide alone. Gastrointestinal events occurred mostly during dose escalation and were more common with the combination.

Where development stands

Lilly says eloralintide has completed Phase 1 and 2 trials and is now in Phase 3 as a single agent for obesity and related conditions such as obstructive sleep apnea and knee osteoarthritis pain. A registered Phase 3 trial of eloralintide in adults with obesity or overweight without type 2 diabetes (NCT07321886) lists an actual start in February 2026 and an estimated primary completion in 2028. Lilly plans to start Phase 3 trials of a co-formulated EloraTZP by the end of 2026. In plain terms, no Phase 3 results exist yet, and regulatory approval is not expected until after those trials report. Timelines in drug development move, and Lilly’s own releases carry the usual warning that the studies may not succeed.

What we do not know

The honest gaps are long. Long-term safety beyond 48 weeks is not published. The Phase 3 results will say far more about rare side effects, durability and what happens when treatment stops. We do not have head-to-head data against the drugs people already use. We do not know how it will be priced or covered, or how a co-formulated product will behave compared with separate injections. All the efficacy numbers above come from company-sponsored trials, reported in company releases and a peer-reviewed paper. That is normal for early drug development, and it is a reason for patience.

Eloralintide and the “research chemical” market

Whenever a candidate drug makes headlines, listings for it appear on research-chemical websites, and eloralintide is no exception. Some sellers describe it in the language of their own catalogs. It is worth being clear about what that means.

There is no approved eloralintide product, and a vial from a research seller is not the Lilly material used in the trials. Whether the powder is eloralintide at all depends entirely on the seller’s documentation, and a molecule this new has few independent reference standards. The trials also used staged dose escalation under medical supervision, and no research listing can tell you how its product would behave in a person. These concerns echo those we raised in our piece on why some people buy from the gray market instead of pharmacies. In short, a trial result describes the trial drug, not anything sold elsewhere.

Common questions

Is eloralintide approved?

No. It is investigational and in Phase 3 trials. It has no approval from the FDA or other regulators.

How is it different from semaglutide or tirzepatide?

It acts on amylin receptors, not GLP-1 or GIP receptors. The combination with tirzepatide was designed to use both pathways.

How much weight did people lose in trials?

In the Phase 2 monotherapy trial, up to about 20% at 48 weeks at the highest dose arm; in the diabetes trial, up to 23.3% for the combination with tirzepatide. These are trial averages under medical supervision, using the more favorable estimand.

Can it be bought legitimately?

Not as a medicine. Only trial participants receive the drug. Anything sold online as eloralintide is an unapproved research product with unverified identity.
